Research

Learn the conversation. Follow the evidence. Let the question evolve.

Research is not the process of finding a required number of sources and forcing them into a paper. It is a process of learning. You begin with a question, discover what scholars already know, encounter new ideas and vocabulary, adjust your thinking, and continue searching.

Good research rarely moves in a perfectly straight line. Expect to revise searches, abandon sources, follow citations, change directions, and discover questions you did not know enough to ask when you began.

Research Is a Process

Start with curiosity, not a conclusion

A topic is not yet a research problem. Strong academic research begins when you identify something that needs to be understood. Instead of beginning with the answer you want to prove, begin with a problem, question, tension, or gap in understanding.

Practical Problems vs. Conceptual Concerns

Practical problems focus on action. They ask what should be changed, fixed, prevented, improved, or done.

Conceptual concerns focus on understanding. They ask why something happens, how different factors interact, or what we need to understand before meaningful action is possible.

Practical:
How can schools reduce bullying?
→
Conceptual:
How do school cultures and policies shape patterns of bullying?

Understanding Comes Before Solutions

Research can eventually help people solve real-world problems, but academic research often begins by asking what we still need to understand about the problem.

The Why Ladder

One way to move from a broad practical problem toward a useful research concern is to keep asking why.

Identify a Practical Problem
Ask Why
Identify What You Do Not Understand
Form a Conceptual Concern
Research for Understanding

The “So What?” Test

A useful research question should matter beyond your personal curiosity. Ask yourself:

If we do not understand this, what larger or more important question remains unanswered?

If your answer reveals a larger consequence, system, debate, relationship, or gap in knowledge, you are moving toward a stronger conceptual research problem.

Is My Topic Ready?

  • My topic raises a meaningful “why” or “how” question.
  • There is something about this issue that requires deeper understanding.
  • The topic is broad enough to support sustained research but narrow enough to become focused.
  • Scholarly research exists on the topic.
  • The topic contains multiple angles, causes, effects, perspectives, or sub-issues.
  • The topic connects to a larger social, cultural, scientific, political, educational, technological, or other system.
  • I am interested enough in the topic to spend significant time reading and thinking about it.

Expect Your Question to Change

Your first research question is a starting point, not a contract. As you read, you will learn new terminology, encounter new perspectives, discover disagreements, and notice issues you did not know existed.

Those discoveries should influence what you search for next. Changing your question because you learned something is not a research failure. It is evidence that research is working.

Searching Like a Researcher

Learn to speak database

People communicate through sentences and questions. Databases work more effectively with concepts, keywords, and relationships between terms.

Instead of typing your entire research question into a database, identify the most important ideas inside the question.

Human Language:
How does food insecurity affect women differently than men in the United States?
→
Database Language:
"food insecurity" AND gender AND "United States"

Break the Question into Concepts

Imagine your research question is:

How does food insecurity affect women differently than men in the United States?

The important concepts might be:

  • food insecurity
  • gender
  • women
  • United States

Words such as how, does, affect, differently, than, and in may help form a readable question, but they are usually not the ideas the database needs.

Use Exact Phrases When They Matter

Quotation marks tell a database to search for words together as a phrase.

food insecurity
→
"food insecurity"

Without quotation marks, a search system may treat the words separately. With quotation marks, you are asking it to look for the specific phrase.

Combine Important Concepts

The word AND tells a database that multiple concepts must appear in the results.

"food insecurity" AND gender

"food insecurity" AND women AND "United States"

"food insecurity" AND caregivers AND gender

Your First Search Is Not Your Last Search

Search terms should evolve as your understanding develops. Scholars may use different terminology than you used when you first described the topic.

Pay attention to words and phrases that repeatedly appear in article titles, abstracts, keywords, and introductions. Those terms can help you search more precisely.

Let the Research Teach You Its Language

If several useful sources repeatedly use a phrase you had never considered, try searching that phrase. The vocabulary used by scholars can lead you deeper into the conversation.

Searching Is Reiterative

Search
Read
Learn New Language
Revise the Search
Search Again

Some searches will produce thousands of results. Some will produce almost nothing. Some will lead to an entirely different angle than the one you expected.

Your job is not to create the perfect search on your first try. Your job is to keep adjusting the search until the results help you understand the conversation you are trying to enter.

Reading & Mining Scholarly Sources

Do not search harder. Follow the conversation.

Scholarly sources do more than provide information. They also point toward the research that shaped the author's thinking. Every useful article can become a map to other researchers, studies, concepts, and conversations.

What Does It Mean to “Mine” a Source?

Mining means using one scholarly source to locate other sources connected to the same academic conversation.

Useful Source
→
Important Idea or Citation
→
Reference Entry
→
New Source
→
New Ideas & Vocabulary

Then the process begins again.

Look at What the Author Cites

When you encounter an important claim, definition, statistic, or idea, look for the citation attached to it. That citation may lead you to the research where the idea was originally developed, tested, or discussed.

Ask Yourself

  • Did this sentence introduce an idea I want to understand better?
  • Is this claim supported by another scholar?
  • Do several authors cite the same study?
  • Does the same scholar keep appearing across multiple sources?
  • Is there terminology here that could improve my next database search?

Notice Repeated Voices

If the same researcher appears in several articles, that may indicate that the scholar has contributed significantly to the conversation.

If several authors cite the same earlier study, that source may represent important or foundational research worth investigating.

Follow Patterns, Not Just Titles

Research becomes easier when you stop treating each article as an isolated object and begin noticing which scholars, concepts, studies, and debates repeatedly connect.

A Source Can Help Even If You Do Not Use It

Sometimes an article is too technical, too narrow, or not useful enough to include in your final project. That does not mean the time spent opening it was wasted.

Its introduction, background section, or discussion of previous scholarship may still help you:

  • learn important vocabulary;
  • identify major researchers;
  • locate foundational studies;
  • discover competing perspectives;
  • find better search terms; or
  • follow references to sources that are more useful.

Research Is Not a Hostage Situation

You are allowed to stop reading a source that is not useful. Read enough to make an informed decision, take anything valuable from it, and move on.

Read Strategically Before Reading Deeply

Before committing to a long scholarly article, determine whether it has a serious chance of helping your research.

  • I read the title carefully.
  • I read the abstract or summary.
  • I reviewed the introduction.
  • I understand the general question or issue the article addresses.
  • The article connects meaningfully to my developing research concern.
  • I identified ideas, terminology, or citations worth following.

After that initial review, decide whether the article deserves deeper reading and annotation.

Choosing & Evaluating Sources

Finding a source is not the same as choosing a source

Search results are possibilities. You still have to decide whether a source is credible, relevant, appropriate for the assignment, and useful to your developing research question.

Different Sources Serve Different Purposes

Not every credible source performs the same job. Some sources provide original evidence. Others interpret that evidence. Some establish background knowledge. Others contribute directly to an ongoing scholarly debate.

Primary Sources

Primary sources provide original or first-hand material. What counts as a primary source depends on the field and the research question.

Secondary Sources

Secondary sources analyze, interpret, discuss, or build from primary material and previous research.

Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed Sources

Scholarly sources are written for an academic audience and participate in a larger research conversation. Peer-reviewed scholarship has also gone through review by other knowledgeable members of the field before publication.

Peer Review Helps, But You Still Have to Think

Peer review gives you an important layer of scholarly vetting. It does not mean every peer-reviewed source is automatically relevant to your question or equally useful for your project.

Do Not Judge Credibility by the URL Alone

A website ending in .org, .edu, or .com gives you only limited information. Credibility requires a closer look.

When evaluating a source, consider:

  • Who is the specific author?
  • What expertise or qualifications does the author have?
  • Where was the source published?
  • When was it published?
  • What evidence does the author use?
  • What other credible sources does the author cite?
  • Can you identify a clear purpose or possible bias?
  • Is the information current enough for your topic?
  • Does the source actually address your research concern?

Before You Read Twenty Pages...

Do some triage first.

  1. Read the title and determine what the article appears to focus on.
  2. Read the abstract to understand the article's purpose, scope, and major focus.
  3. Read enough of the introduction to determine whether the discussion connects to your research.
  4. Look at the article type and make sure it meets the requirements of your assignment.
  5. Scan the references and notice what scholarship the article builds upon.
  6. Decide whether deeper reading is worth your time.

Long Does Not Mean Bad

Do not reject a useful scholarly article simply because it is longer than you hoped. Research requires reading, and stronger projects usually come from deeper engagement with good sources.

At the same time, you do not need to read every source from beginning to end simply because you opened it. Strategic reading helps you decide where your time will matter most.

Do Not Build Your Research Around Convenience

Choosing only the shortest or easiest sources may save time at the beginning, but it can make writing much harder later. Your argument can only become as developed as the research supporting it.

Research Tools, AI & Your Own Thinking

Supplement your skills. Do not replace them.

Research tools can help you locate, organize, and explore scholarship. They can make parts of the process faster, but speed is not the same thing as understanding.

Every tool should support your judgment rather than make your decisions for you.

Academic Databases

Databases help you search collections of scholarly and academic materials using keywords, phrases, filters, and other search strategies.

Your job: develop useful search language, evaluate results, read sources, and continue refining the search.

Google Scholar

Google Scholar can help you locate scholarly material and follow citations beyond your initial database searches.

Your job: verify the source, determine whether you can access it, and evaluate whether it is appropriate for your project.

Citation-Network Tools

Tools that map related scholarship can help you visualize connections between authors, studies, and related works.

Your job: use those connections as leads, then locate, evaluate, and read the actual scholarship.

Generative AI

AI tools may help brainstorm search language, identify possible directions, or suggest scholarship worth investigating.

Your job: verify everything. Never assume a source exists, is scholarly, says what the AI claims, or meets the requirements of your assignment until you locate and evaluate it yourself.

AI Is Not the Source

If an AI system suggests a source, that suggestion is only a lead. You must locate the actual article, verify that it exists, determine what kind of source it is, and read it yourself.

Replacing:
Ask AI for ten sources, accept the list, and build the paper around them.
→
Supplementing:
Use AI to generate possible leads or search language, then locate, evaluate, read, and choose sources yourself.

Research Tools Should Expand Your Agency

The purpose of research is not merely to produce a finished document. Research teaches you how to ask better questions, identify patterns, evaluate information, recognize scholarly conversations, and form your own informed position.

The Rule to Remember

A research tool can help you find the door. You still have to open it and go inside.

If a tool is doing so much of the intellectual work that you no longer understand how the research developed, the tool is no longer supplementing your research skills. It is replacing them.
