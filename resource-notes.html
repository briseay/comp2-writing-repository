Resource Notes

Internal Composition II coaching language used to guide writing, research, revision, and source-use support. This page is intentionally unlinked from the student-facing Writing Center.

Use of This Reference

Use these notes to mirror Mrs. Seay's coaching language, expectations, and questioning style when helping students with Composition II work.

Do not assign grades, estimate grades, apply point deductions, invent assignment requirements, or claim that a specific error exists unless that error is actually present in the student's writing.

When an assignment prompt, rubric, instructor comment, or course material is available, those materials take priority over this reference.

Use feedback as coaching. Help students identify the concern, understand why it matters, and decide what they should revise. Do not simply rewrite the student's work for them.

Common Feedback I Leave

Writing Process & Mindset

  • Academic writing is a process of constant learning. Feedback should help you identify what is working, what is unclear, and what needs further development.
  • Marks that do not have an attached comment mean that the concern has been addressed or explained elsewhere in the paper.
  • Feedback on a draft is foundational rather than all-inclusive. Apply the explanation to similar concerns throughout the rest of the paper.
  • Revision should focus first on content, logic, organization, and development before moving to smaller editing concerns.
  • A strong revision does more than correct marked sentences. It uses the feedback to improve the larger writing habits that created the concern.

Coaching Direction

Encourage students to slow down, identify the actual problem, and revise the idea or logic rather than merely editing surface-level wording.

Common Feedback I Leave

Academic Tone & Conventions

  • Do not use second person in academic arguments. It is informal and can become accusatory because the audience cannot respond directly.
  • Avoid first-person personal and collective pronouns unless the assignment specifically calls for reflection.
  • Avoid first-person collective pronouns such as we, our, and us. You cannot speak for an undefined group of people.
  • Avoid all-inclusive wording such as always, never, everyone, all, and no one. These claims usually exceed what can be proven within the scope of a paper.
  • Do not use contractions in formal academic writing.
  • Do not ask rhetorical questions in academic papers. Instead of asking the question, state the answer you want the reader to understand.
  • Avoid metadiscourse. There is no need to tell readers what you are about to discuss, what you are currently discussing, or what you have already discussed.
  • Avoid weak modifiers such as very, really, a lot, and just. Often the word can be removed or replaced with a clearer choice.
  • Avoid clichés, colloquialisms, and phrasing that sounds conversational rather than academic.
  • Be careful with evaluative or emotionally loaded language, especially in argumentative writing. Readers who disagree with you should still feel invited to seriously consider your reasoning.
  • Authors can discuss, argue, claim, explain, examine, evaluate, conclude, or report. Avoid saying that written articles “talk,” “speak,” or “say.”
  • Connect ideas to the author rather than the article when describing an intellectual action. Articles are inanimate objects.

Introductions & Conclusions

Introductions

  • Open with a discussion slightly broader than the thesis so there is space to establish necessary context before narrowing the paper.
  • The introduction should move from broad to narrow and lead naturally toward the paper's central focus.
  • Do not use evidence or direct quotations in the introduction unless the assignment specifically requires them.
  • Too much summary in the introduction can bury the contextual work the opening should be doing.
  • An introduction may contain the required pieces but still need revision if those pieces do not create a coherent introductory conversation.

Conclusions

  • A conclusion should move beyond summarizing what the paper already discussed.
  • Look at the larger picture and ask what all of the developed ideas show when viewed together.
  • Focus on significance, implications, unresolved questions, or the larger conversation rather than repeating body paragraph content.
  • Do not introduce new evidence or direct quotations in the conclusion.
  • The conclusion should feel connected to the paper rather than introducing a new focus that appeared nowhere in the body.

Useful Conclusion Question

What do all of these ideas, when viewed together, allow the reader to understand now?

Common Feedback I Leave

Claims, Thesis & Focus

  • Each body paragraph should begin with a clear point that establishes what that paragraph is responsible for developing.
  • Do not begin the paragraph with evidence. Begin with your own leading idea, then use research to support it.
  • A paragraph should not attempt to prove multiple unrelated claims at once.
  • Strong claims should build together into one coherent larger argument rather than functioning as separate mini-essays.
  • A topic sentence should identify the specific focus of the paragraph, not merely name the general topic.
  • Ask “to what end?” What larger understanding is this point helping the paper develop?

Common Feedback I Leave

Organization & Transitions

  • Internal transition needed. These ideas do not logically follow one another without additional explanation.
  • External transition needed. Connect the main point of the previous paragraph to the focus of the new paragraph.
  • A transition should explain the relationship between ideas, not merely announce that the paper is moving to a new topic.
  • Do not repeat evidence or reasoning. Each new sentence should add significance rather than restating what has already been explained.
  • Several sentences use the same opening structure. This can make the writing feel choppy and rigid.
  • Streamline these thoughts. They can be combined and revised to express the same information more clearly and concisely.
  • Clunky phrasing or organization is making the idea harder to read than it needs to be.
  • Paragraphs may address the same general subject but still need stronger connections to form one cohesive paper.

Evidence & Source Integration

  • Evidence should prove your point rather than merely show that another person agrees with you.
  • Evidence that repeats the claim does not prove the claim. It only establishes that someone else has made a similar statement.
  • Use specific evidence that supports the discussion without overshadowing your own voice.
  • This evidence is related to the topic but is too vague to function as useful support for the specific point being developed.
  • Do not open or close a body paragraph with quoted or paraphrased evidence. The first and last voices in the paragraph should normally be yours.
  • A direct quotation cannot stand alone. Integrate it through tagging or blending.
  • Use a comma when a quotation follows a verb of expression such as wrote, noted, explained, or commented.
  • Do not use a comma when that blends the quotation directly into your sentence.
  • Do not use ellipses at the beginning or end of a quotation simply to show that material existed before or after the selected passage.
  • When material inside the original source already contains quotation marks, use single quotation marks inside your quotation.

The Double Down Rule

After using evidence, double down. If the evidence takes roughly one sentence, spend at least two sentences explaining, interpreting, breaking down, connecting, or extending that evidence before moving to the next source.

The goal is not a perfect mathematical ratio. The goal is to keep the writer's reasoning louder than the evidence.

Common Feedback I Leave

Reasoning & Analysis

  • Explain how. The foundation of the idea may be present, but readers should not be expected to fill in the logic because it feels obvious to the writer.
  • Explain why the claim should be taken seriously.
  • The reasoning is underdeveloped because the conversation stops before fully explaining the implications of the evidence.
  • Reasoning should link the claim and evidence together in a way that demonstrates why the evidence actually matters.
  • Do not simply repeat the evidence after presenting it. Explain its significance.
  • Avoid phrases such as “this shows” when they lead into a sentence that simply restates the quotation. Move directly into the analysis.
  • Before moving forward, explain why the example functions in the way you claim and what larger understanding follows from it.
  • Ask what logical step you are asking the reader to make. If that step is invisible, explain it.

Composition II Coaching

Research & Synthesis

  • Research should develop your understanding rather than simply help you locate evidence for a conclusion you already decided was true.
  • A source does not need to stand alone. Group sources that address the same idea, question, trend, or problem.
  • Synthesis means showing what multiple sources reveal when viewed together.
  • Do not structure research writing as Source A, then Source B, then Source C when those sources belong to the same scholarly discussion.
  • Look for agreement, disagreement, extensions, complications, repeated researchers, and repeated concepts.
  • Strong scholarly evidence often appears in the results, discussion, and conclusion sections rather than in abstracts or introductory literature reviews.
  • Introductory sections and literature reviews can still be valuable for understanding the conversation, learning terminology, and mining citations.
  • Evidence should support the conversation being developed. A piece of information can be true and still be too vague or disconnected to function effectively.

Synthesis Questions

  • What do these sources show when read together?
  • Which sources belong in the same conversation?
  • Where do the sources agree?
  • Where do they complicate one another?
  • Does one source help explain another?
  • What larger pattern is becoming visible?

Assignment-Specific Feedback

Literature Review Feedback

Overall Purpose

  • A literature review should present and synthesize the research conversation rather than argue a personal position.
  • Avoid evaluative wording such as good, bad, better, worst, or similar language that shifts the review toward argumentation.
  • Topic sentences should introduce what researchers are examining without announcing the findings before the summaries do that work.
  • Wrap-up sentences should show what the grouped research reveals when viewed together rather than repeat the topic sentence or simply summarize the summaries.
  • A source should not stand alone when the assignment is asking for synthesis. Group related research into thematic paragraphs.

Condensed Summaries

Three-Sentence Structure

  1. Main point or focus of the source
  2. Main findings or results
  3. Main conclusion, significance, implication, or future direction
  • Each condensed summary should maintain the required balance rather than spending disproportionate space on one source.
  • Every sentence in a pure summary should remain clearly connected to the author through the name, pronoun, or an attribution phrase.
  • Without attribution, source ideas can read as if they belong to the student.
  • Do not merely tell the reader that an author provided information. Summarize what that information actually was.
  • A vague summary does not give readers enough information to understand what the research actually found.
  • Make sure the summary includes the source's conclusion or significance. Without it, the summary often feels unfinished.
  • Use present tense when introducing what research is examining, but past tense may be appropriate when summarizing the completed work of a specific study.

Pure Summary and Source Wording

A pure summary should use the student's own wording and sentence structure. Changing only a few words from the original is paraphrasing, not summarizing.

If source wording is too close to the original, the student should rewrite the information rather than merely add quotation marks when the assignment requires pure summary.

Introduction

  • The literature review introduction should provide context for the topic rather than begin making the later argumentative claim.
  • Introduce major categories, themes, trends, or research directions that organize the review.
  • Develop enough context before the category breakdown for readers to understand the research landscape.

Conclusion

  • The conclusion should focus on what the reviewed research collectively shows.
  • Address gaps in this review or unresolved areas of understanding, not simply practical problems outside the scope of the reviewed scholarship.
  • Explain what the review helps readers understand rather than turning the conclusion into an argumentative section.

Assignment-Specific Feedback

Definition Assignment Feedback

  • The goal is not merely to provide a dictionary-style definition. Explain how the concept functions inside the larger research topic.
  • Use an academic source to establish the definition.
  • The evidence should appear early enough in the paragraph to anchor the discussion, but the paragraph should still begin with the writer's own leading idea.
  • Explain misunderstandings or competing understandings surrounding the term.
  • Discuss how that misunderstanding creates or contributes to a larger social or conceptual problem.
  • Do not shift into first-person explanation of how the writer plans to use the term later in the paper.
  • Avoid turning the paragraph into a miniature argumentative essay about the larger topic. Keep the focus on understanding the term and the consequences of misunderstanding it.
  • If two selected terms are largely redundant, consider whether one should be replaced with a concept that adds a different layer of understanding.

Questions to Push the Definition

  • What does this term mean in the scholarly conversation?
  • Where does misunderstanding occur?
  • Why does that misunderstanding matter?
  • How does it affect the larger issue being researched?

Argumentative Essay Feedback

Conceptual Concern

  • The argument should focus on a conceptual concern: something society does not adequately understand about the issue.
  • Avoid replacing the conceptual concern with a purely practical solution-focused argument.
  • The thesis should identify one clear central concern rather than setting up an expository discussion.

Body Paragraphs

  • Claims should directly support the thesis and build toward the larger argument.
  • Evidence should prove the claim rather than simply provide another source making the same statement.
  • Replace repetitive evidence with more specific evidence when possible.
  • Avoid data dumps. Double down after every piece of evidence so the writer's reasoning remains central.
  • Warrants should explain the logic connecting the evidence to the claim.
  • When the paper is underdeveloped, look carefully at the warrant: what consequence, implication, or logical connection has not yet been explained?

Conclusion

  • Do not simply summarize the claims from the body.
  • Explain why the conceptual concern matters and what larger implications follow if the problem in understanding remains unresolved.
  • Make sure the conclusion remains connected to ideas actually developed in the paper.

Argument Questions

  • What exactly is the claim?
  • Why should a skeptical reader take it seriously?
  • How does this evidence prove the point?
  • What assumption connects the evidence and the claim?
  • What happens if the misunderstanding remains unresolved?

Assignment-Specific Feedback

Alternative Viewpoint Feedback

  • The alternative viewpoint should represent a credible scholarly position that genuinely tilts away from the paper's argument.
  • Clearly attribute the alternative position to another scholar or perspective so the paragraph does not sound like the writer is arguing against themself.
  • Explain the alternative viewpoint fairly before responding to it.
  • Use evidence to establish why the alternative viewpoint deserves consideration.
  • An acknowledgement without a developed response can weaken the argument because the opposing position is left unanswered.
  • The response must address the same issue raised by the alternative viewpoint.
  • Use evidence in the response when needed. A rebuttal should not rely on unsupported dismissal.
  • Fully discuss the final evidence before ending the paragraph so the refutation feels complete.

Alternative Viewpoint Questions

  • Whose position is this?
  • Why might a reasonable reader accept it?
  • What evidence supports that viewpoint?
  • Where does your response disagree or complicate it?
  • Does your response address the exact same issue?
  • What evidence supports your response?

Assignment-Specific Coaching

Research Presentation Coaching

  • The presentation should demonstrate understanding rather than simply reproduce the written paper.
  • Focus on what the audience needs to understand most rather than attempting to include every source or every detail.
  • Explain the research in language that an audience can follow without oversimplifying the ideas.
  • Use the presentation to show how the major parts of the research connect.
  • A student who understands the research should be able to explain why the findings matter and respond to reasonable questions about the topic.
  • Discussion and questions can reveal gaps in understanding that were less visible while writing.

Presentation Questions

  • If you had five minutes, what does your audience most need to know?
  • What larger research conversation does this connect to?
  • What finding or idea matters most?
  • Why does it matter?
  • What question might your audience ask next?

Common Feedback I Leave

Grammar & Sentence Clarity

  • Word choice concern: the selected word may be inaccurate, awkward, or carry a connotation that does not match the intended tone.
  • Revise for concision. The same idea can be expressed in fewer words without losing meaning.
  • Subject-verb agreement: singular subjects require singular verbs; plural subjects require plural verbs.
  • Maintain consistent verb tense unless there is a clear reason to shift.
  • Use an introductory comma when a dependent clause or phrase appears before the independent clause.
  • A comma is usually not needed when the dependent clause or phrase appears at the end of the sentence.
  • Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins two independent clauses.
  • Do not use a comma merely because a coordinating conjunction creates a compound subject or predicate.
  • A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined with only a comma.
  • A run-on occurs when independent clauses are joined without proper punctuation or connection.
  • A semicolon connects two independent clauses.
  • When a conjunctive adverb joins two independent clauses, use a semicolon before the conjunctive adverb and a comma after it.
  • A fragment is a phrase or dependent clause attempting to stand alone as a complete sentence.
  • Pronouns should have clear antecedents and agree with them in number.
  • Parallel ideas should use parallel grammatical structures.
  • Passive voice can hide the actor. Revise to active voice when naming the actor would improve clarity.
  • Appositives and nonrestrictive clauses generally require commas around the nonessential information.
  • Be careful with prepositions when a particular word requires a specific prepositional relationship.

Common Feedback I Leave

APA & Source Use

  • Every source-based idea should be clearly connected to the source through correct APA citation.
  • Narrative citations use the author's name in the sentence. Parenthetical citations place the author and year in parentheses.
  • With two authors, spell out and in narrative citations and use & inside parenthetical citations.
  • Direct quotations require a page number or another appropriate locator when available.
  • Paraphrases and summaries still require citation.
  • Article titles in References entries use sentence case.
  • Journal titles use title case.
  • Do not attribute a source to an editor when no individual author is listed. Follow the appropriate no-author citation pattern.
  • References entries should be alphabetized by the first author or first identifying element.
  • Do not rearrange the authors inside an individual source.
  • Use a DOI when available. If no DOI is available and a URL is required, use the appropriate URL.
  • References entries should be double-spaced and use hanging indents without extra blank space between entries.
  • Check APA formatting carefully before submission. Formatting, citations, and References entries should work together as one documentation system.

Source Attribution

Listing a source on the References page does not identify which ideas came from that source inside the paper. Source-based material must still be cited where it appears.

Revision Coaching Questions

  • How so?
  • Why does this matter?
  • To what end?
  • What exactly is this paragraph trying to establish?
  • How does this evidence prove the claim?

  • What logical step are you asking the reader to make?

  • Are these two sentences logically connected?

  • Is this describing the source, or actually summarizing its ideas?
  • What did the researchers actually find?
  • Why did the authors think those findings were significant?
  • What do these sources show when viewed together?
  • Where is the connection between these sources?

  • What makes this source useful beyond simply being peer reviewed?

  • Whose alternative viewpoint is being represented?
  • Does the response address the same issue raised by the opposing source?
  • What can the reader understand at the end that they could not understand at the beginning?

Common Feedback I Leave

Positive Feedback Language

General

  • The ideas are solid, and there is a clear presence of critical thinking.
  • Your overall tone is professional and appropriate for this type of academic writing.
  • The organization makes the discussion easy to follow.
  • You have a strong foundation here and the major components are functioning effectively.
  • The revision addressed the major concerns and made this section substantially stronger.

Literature Review

  • You maintain clear connections to the authors throughout, so the material consistently reads as summary rather than argument.
  • The summaries are specific enough to give readers a clear sense of what each source found.
  • The topic sentences introduce the research focus without prematurely announcing the findings.
  • The wrap-up effectively shows what the grouped research reveals when viewed together.
  • The sources are grouped in a way that demonstrates clear synthesis.

Argumentative Writing

  • The claims clearly support the thesis and create a coherent progression through the argument.
  • The evidence supports the claims without overshadowing your own reasoning.
  • Your warrants clearly explain the logic connecting the evidence to the claim.
  • The alternative viewpoint is fairly represented and the response directly addresses the same issue.
  • The rebuttal is balanced, complete, and supported on both sides of the conversation.
  • The conclusion moves beyond summary and effectively explains why the argument matters.

Research & Source Use

  • The sources work together instead of functioning as isolated pieces of evidence.
  • You selected evidence that actually proves the discussion rather than merely repeating it.
  • Your explanation after the evidence keeps your own voice central to the paragraph.
  • APA citations and References entries are accurate and consistent.
