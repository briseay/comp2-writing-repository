Composition II

Writing

Research gives you information. Writing gives that information direction. Strong research-based writing does more than report what sources say: it develops your own ideas, uses evidence deliberately, connects sources to one another, and explains why the information matters.

Your Voice Should Lead the Paper

Sources add evidence and credibility, but they should support your thinking rather than replace it. Your reader should leave the paper understanding what you learned, connected, interpreted, and concluded from the research.

Academic Writing & Voice

Be formal without disappearing

Academic writing values precision, clarity, objectivity, and control. Formal writing does not mean using unnecessarily large words or sounding unlike yourself. It means making deliberate language choices that keep the focus on the ideas.

Common Academic Conventions

  • Avoid first-person phrases such as “I think” or “I believe.”
  • Avoid generalized second person such as “you” or “your.”
  • Write contractions in their complete form.
  • Avoid slang, clichés, and overly conversational wording.
  • Make direct statements instead of asking rhetorical questions.
  • Avoid exaggerated or unnecessarily emotional language.
  • Refer to authors formally, normally by last name.
Less Direct:
I think the study proves that food insecurity affects women differently.
→
More Direct:
The study demonstrates that food insecurity affects women differently.

Formal Does Not Mean Lifeless

Your academic voice comes from the claims you make, the evidence you choose, the connections you notice, and the conclusions you draw. Removing “I think” does not remove you from the paper.

Building Academic Paragraphs

Give every paragraph a job

A strong academic paragraph develops one focused idea. The paragraph should not simply collect information that happens to relate to the topic.

Claim What idea is this paragraph developing?
Development What does the reader need to understand first?
Evidence What information supports the idea?
Analysis Why does that evidence matter?

Start with the Point, Not the Source

A body paragraph should normally begin with your own topic sentence. Do not allow a quotation to decide what the paragraph is about.

Source-Led Paragraph

“Food insecurity affects women at disproportionately high rates” (Brown, 2022, p. 18). This shows that food insecurity affects women.

Writer-Led Paragraph

Gender can shape how food insecurity is experienced within households. Brown (2022) found that “food insecurity affects women at disproportionately high rates” (p. 18). This disparity suggests that household food access cannot be understood only as an economic issue because social roles may also influence who absorbs the greatest effects of scarcity.

Ask One Question

If you cannot explain what a paragraph is trying to prove, clarify the paragraph before adding more evidence.

Using Evidence Effectively

Evidence must actually prove something

A source can be credible and still provide poor evidence for a particular claim. Good evidence must be relevant to the specific point you are making.

Evidence Is Not Decoration

Do not add a quotation simply because it sounds intelligent, dramatic, or vaguely related to the topic. Ask what the information actually demonstrates.

Related:
The source discusses the general topic.
→
Relevant:
The source provides information that directly supports the claim being developed.

Evidence Does Not Speak for Itself

Readers should never be expected to determine why a quotation, statistic, study, or paraphrase proves your point. That explanation belongs to the writer.

After Every Piece of Evidence, Ask:

  • What does this information show?
  • Why is it important?
  • How does it support my claim?
  • What can I infer from it?
  • How does it connect to the other research?

Look for Original Evidence

When working with research studies, distinguish between the article's discussion of previous research and the findings of the study itself.

An introduction or literature review can be extremely useful for understanding the scholarly conversation and mining other sources. However, when you need evidence from a study, look carefully at the results and discussion where the authors present and interpret their own findings.

Quote, Paraphrase & Summary

Choose the form that fits the job

Research can enter your writing in several ways. The best choice depends on what you need the source to accomplish.

Quote

Uses the author's exact words.

Quote when the wording is especially important, memorable, authoritative, or worthy of analysis.

Paraphrase

Restates a specific passage or idea fully in your own wording and sentence structure.

Paraphrase when the information matters more than the author's exact phrasing.

Summary

Condenses a larger section or source into its central ideas.

Summarize when the reader needs an overview rather than individual details.

All Three Require Citation

Changing the wording does not make the information yours. Quotations, paraphrases, and summaries all come from another source and must be credited.

Paraphrasing Means More Than Replacing Words

A true paraphrase changes both the wording and the sentence structure while preserving the source's meaning. Simply swapping several words for synonyms is not enough.

Do Not Overuse Any Form of Source Material

Research-based writing still needs to sound like your paper. If most of the paragraph consists of quoted, paraphrased, or summarized material, the sources have begun to crowd out your own thinking.

Integrating Evidence

Do not drop a source into the paragraph and walk away

Source material should fit naturally into your sentence and paragraph. Readers need a bridge between your thinking and the source's contribution.

The Dropped Quote

Dropped Quote

Food insecurity creates different challenges across demographic groups. “Women experience food insecurity at higher rates than men” (Brown, 2021, p. 93).

The quotation appears without a grammatical or rhetorical connection to the writer's sentence. The reader is left to figure out why it has suddenly appeared.

Tagging with a Signal Phrase

Tagged Evidence

According to Brown (2021), “women experience food insecurity at higher rates than men” (p. 93).

A signal phrase introduces the author and creates a clear bridge into the evidence.

Blending Evidence into Your Sentence

Blended Evidence

Gender is one factor shaping access to food because “women experience food insecurity at higher rates than men” (Brown, 2021, p. 93).

Blending makes the source part of the writer's own grammatical sentence. Both tagging and blending are useful, and strong research writing uses variety.

Quotes Must Fit Complete Sentences

Whether you tag or blend, the quotation must function grammatically inside the sentence. Read the entire sentence aloud without mentally separating the quoted words from your own.

Using Ellipses and Brackets

Ellipsis (...) can show that words have been omitted from the middle of quoted material.

Brackets [ ] can show a small change made for grammar or clarity, such as adjusting a pronoun.

Neither tool may be used to change the author's original meaning.

The Double Down Rule

Evidence begins the thinking. It does not end it.

Double Down

If a piece of evidence takes approximately one sentence, spend at least two sentences discussing, explaining, evaluating, connecting, or interpreting that evidence before moving to another source.

Double Down is not a mathematical law. It is a habit designed to keep your writing from becoming a chain of quotations.

What Can I Do After the Evidence?

Explain

Clarify what the evidence demonstrates.

Analyze

Identify why the information matters to your claim.

Infer

Draw a reasonable conclusion from the evidence.

Connect

Relate the evidence to another idea or source.

Complicate

Explain a limitation, tension, or exception.

Extend

Use the evidence to develop the discussion further.

Evidence:
Brown (2021) found a measurable gender disparity in food insecurity.
→
Double Down:
This disparity suggests that food insecurity is shaped by more than household income alone. Gendered caregiving and resource-distribution expectations may influence who experiences scarcity most directly.

Your Voice Should Be Louder

The quotation gives the paper support. Your explanation shows the reader that you understand what the support means.

Synthesis

Put sources in conversation

Synthesis happens when you organize writing around ideas rather than around individual sources. Instead of reporting what each scholar says one at a time, you use multiple sources to develop one part of the conversation.

Source-by-Source Writing

What Synthesis Is Not

Brown studies gender and food insecurity. Brown finds that women experience greater food insecurity. Smith studies caregiving. Smith explains that women perform more caregiving labor. Jones studies household resources. Jones finds that household resources are not distributed evenly.

The sources are all present, but they are standing beside one another rather than interacting.

Idea-Centered Writing

Brown
Gender disparities in food insecurity
Smith
Gendered expectations surrounding caregiving
Jones
Unequal distribution of resources within households
Synthesized Discussion

Gender disparities in food insecurity may be connected to expectations surrounding women's roles inside households. While Brown identifies higher rates of food insecurity among women, Smith's research on caregiving suggests that women are also more likely to carry responsibility for meeting other family members' needs. Jones further complicates this pattern by demonstrating that household resources are not always distributed evenly. Together, these findings suggest that individual food insecurity can be shaped by social roles within the household, not simply by the total amount of food or income available.

Look for Relationships Between Sources

  • Do they agree?
  • Does one source extend another?
  • Do they study different parts of the same problem?
  • Do they reach conflicting conclusions?
  • Does one help explain the findings of another?
  • Does one introduce a limitation or complication?

The Paragraph Belongs to the Idea

Do not ask, “What does Source A say?” Ask, “What does this paragraph need to explain, and which sources help me explain it?”

Transitions & Connections

Show the relationship, not just the movement

Transitions help readers understand how ideas, sentences, and paragraphs connect. Good transitions make the logic of the paper visible.

External Transitions

External transitions connect major ideas across paragraphs. Instead of simply announcing that the topic has changed, they explain the relationship between the old idea and the new one.

Weak:
Another thing researchers study is household labor.
→
Stronger:
While economic access contributes to food insecurity, household labor expectations may further explain why its effects are not evenly distributed.

Internal Transitions

Internal transitions guide readers from sentence to sentence within a paragraph.

Agreement: similarly, likewise, in addition

Contrast: however, in contrast, while

Cause or result: therefore, consequently, as a result

Example: for example, specifically

Complication: although, despite this, nevertheless

A Transition Word Cannot Fix Broken Logic

Adding “however” does not create a contrast unless the ideas actually contrast. Decide what relationship exists first, then choose language that makes that relationship clear.

Proofread Between Paragraphs

Read the final sentence of one paragraph and the first sentence of the next together. If the connection is unclear, revise the transition, clarify the logic, or reconsider the paragraph order.

Writing Introductions

Move from the larger conversation toward your focus

An introduction establishes what the paper is about, gives the reader necessary context, narrows toward the central issue, and presents the controlling argument or purpose.

Introduction: General → Specific

Larger Topic or Context
Relevant Background
Focus / Thesis

What the Reader Should Know

  • What larger issue the paper addresses
  • What context is necessary
  • What specific problem or question matters
  • What argument or controlling idea the paper develops

Things to Avoid

The Dictionary Opening

Webster's Dictionary defines...

The Assignment Restatement

This assignment asks students to research...

The Announcement

In this paper, I will be discussing...

Do Not Write a Mystery Novel

Academic readers should not have to wait several pages to discover what the paper is actually arguing. Be clear about where the paper is going.

Writing Conclusions

Move from your findings back toward the bigger picture

A conclusion brings together the strands developed throughout the paper and explains what those ideas allow the reader to understand.

Conclusion: Specific → General

Return to the Main Finding
Explain Significance
Connect to the Bigger Picture

What the Conclusion Should Do

  • Bring the developed ideas together
  • Return to the paper's central purpose
  • Explain significance or implications
  • Connect to the larger academic conversation

Do Not Simply Repeat the Introduction

The reader has now traveled through the entire paper. A conclusion should reflect what has been learned or established, not merely restate the thesis using slightly different words.

No New Evidence

The conclusion is not the place to introduce another study, quotation, or major claim. The evidence belongs in the body, where you have room to explain it.

Introduction:
What does the reader need to understand before entering this discussion?
↔
Conclusion:
What can the reader understand now that the discussion has been developed?

Revising Research-Based Writing

Check the thinking, not just the commas

Editing focuses on correctness. Revision asks whether the paper actually works. Before polishing individual sentences, make sure the ideas, evidence, and relationships between sources are doing what you intended.

Research-Based Writing Checklist

  • My introduction establishes the context and clear focus of the paper.
  • Each body paragraph develops one identifiable idea.
  • My topic sentences begin with my ideas rather than quotations.
  • Every piece of evidence directly supports the point around it.
  • I integrate source material rather than dropping it into paragraphs.
  • I explain and analyze evidence after using it.
  • My voice remains more prominent than the voices of my sources.
  • I use quoting, paraphrasing, and summarizing deliberately.
  • I bring multiple sources together when they address the same idea.
  • Transitions explain relationships between ideas.
  • My conclusion explains significance rather than simply repeating the paper.
  • I have cited all borrowed words, information, and ideas.

The Final Question

If the sources disappeared from the page, could a reader still identify your thinking?

If not, the paper probably needs more analysis, synthesis, explanation, or connection between ideas.
