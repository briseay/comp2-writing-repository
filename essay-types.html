Composition II

Major Assignments

The major assignments in Composition II are not isolated projects. Each one asks you to do something new with the same developing body of research. As the semester progresses, you move from learning the scholarly conversation to defining its terms, building your own argument, and finally communicating what you know to other people.

How the Assignments Connect

Research develops in stages. First, you learn what others have discovered. Then you clarify important concepts, decide where you stand, and communicate your understanding to an audience.

Literature Review: Understand the Conversation

Learn what scholars are already saying

A literature review discusses published research on a focused topic. Its purpose is not to introduce new research or argue your personal position. Instead, it helps you understand what scholars have already discovered, where they agree, where they disagree, and what questions remain.

The Goal

The literature review helps you understand how your research question fits into the ongoing academic conversation.

Annotation Means Better Reading

Scholarly articles can be dense. Annotation forces you to slow down, process difficult ideas, and respond to the text rather than simply moving your eyes across the page.

Clarify

Define difficult terms or concepts.

Connect

Relate the reading to your question or another source.

Question

Ask about assumptions, methods, or implications.

Summarize

Capture the main point of a section.

Interpret

Explain what a result or claim might mean.

Compare

Notice where sources agree, differ, or overlap.

Weak Annotation

Interesting.

Strong Annotation

Connect: This supports my focus on rural digital access. Compare this result with the source that discusses device sharing in households.

Writing Literature Review Summaries

Literature review summaries are short and focused. Their job is to report the source's main idea, findings, or conclusion clearly and neutrally.

  • Focus only on the most important findings or conclusions.
  • Use your own words.
  • Avoid personal opinion.
  • Avoid direct quotations.
  • Use signal verbs such as finds, argues, reports, explains, or concludes.
  • Make it clear that the ideas belong to the source.

Think Like a Reporter

Your job in a literature review summary is to represent the source accurately and fairly, not to explain whether you agree with it.

Synthesis: Organize by Ideas, Not Sources

Literature reviews become more useful when sources are grouped according to shared themes, findings, debates, or patterns. Instead of writing one paragraph about Source A and one paragraph about Source B, organize the review around what the research is collectively showing.

Writing the Literature Review Introduction

A literature review introduction establishes the topic and helps readers understand the research landscape they are about to enter.

  • Identify the general topic.
  • Provide relevant context.
  • Identify major trends or categories in the research.
  • Note meaningful conflicts or gaps when appropriate.
  • Explain the focus or purpose of the review.
  • Present a thesis or statement of focus.

A Literature Review Thesis Is Different

The thesis of a literature review often states the review's focus or purpose rather than making an argumentative claim.

Writing the Literature Review Conclusion

The conclusion should not simply repeat each source. Instead, bring together what the body of scholarship collectively shows.

  • Summarize the major patterns across the research.
  • Identify meaningful gaps or inconsistencies.
  • Explain what the literature allows us to understand.
  • Connect the topic back to the larger academic conversation.

Definition Assignment: Clarify the Conversation

Make sure everyone is using the same language

Academic arguments become difficult when writers and readers use the same word but mean different things by it. Some concepts are layered, subjective, contested, or dependent on context.

The Goal

Clarify the important terms and concepts that your audience must understand before the larger research conversation can make sense.

Why Definitions Matter

Terms such as poverty, universal healthcare, free speech, or climate change may appear straightforward, but people can apply them differently depending on context.

When an important concept is ambiguous, readers may think they disagree with one another when they are actually working from different definitions.

What Should Be Defined?

  • The term is central to your topic.
  • The term has more than one accepted meaning.
  • The concept may be unfamiliar to your audience.
  • The meaning changes depending on field, culture, policy, or context.
  • Misunderstanding the term would weaken the argument.

Go Beyond the Dictionary

Research definitions should help explain how a term functions inside the specific conversation you are studying. A simple dictionary entry may not capture its disciplinary, legal, cultural, medical, or social meaning.

Define for Clarity

The purpose is not to prove that your definition is the only possible one. It is to make clear how the concept is being used in your research so the audience understands the same conversation you are trying to enter.

Connect the Definition to the Research

A strong definition section does more than provide a quotation. It explains why the concept matters and how that meaning affects the issue being studied.

Argumentative Essay: Enter the Conversation

Use the research to develop your own position

By the time you reach the argumentative essay, you have already spent significant time learning the research conversation. Now you begin contributing your own reasoned position to it.

The Goal

Present a claim that reasonable people could debate, support that claim with strong reasons and evidence, explain the logic connecting them, and engage fairly with alternative viewpoints.

The Core of an Academic Argument

Claim
Reasons
Evidence
Warrant
Response

Claims

A claim is the position you are asking your audience to seriously consider. Strong claims are clear, specific, supportable, and debatable by reasonable people.

Do Not Argue a Fact

If no reasonable disagreement exists, you may have a statement of fact rather than an argumentative claim.

Reasons

Reasons explain why your claim deserves consideration. They are the major “whys” supporting the thesis-level position.

Imagine that your reader does not automatically agree with you. That forces you to choose stronger reasons and provide better evidence.

Warrants

A warrant explains the underlying belief or logic connecting a reason and the claim.

Warrants become especially important when the audience is unfamiliar with the topic, resistant to the reason, or when the reasoning depends on a debated assumption.

Backing

Backing provides additional support for the warrant. If your audience might question the assumption connecting your evidence to your claim, backing helps show why that connection is reasonable.

Qualifiers

Qualifiers place honest limits on a claim. Words such as many, some, often, or in certain situations can make an argument stronger by preventing the writer from claiming more than the evidence can support.

Limit the Claim When Necessary

If the evidence does not support “always,” do not claim “always.” Precision strengthens credibility.

Alternative Viewpoints

An alternative viewpoint is a credible perspective that challenges or complicates your argument. It does not have to claim the exact opposite of your position.

Strong academic writers demonstrate that they understand these perspectives before responding to them.

Introduce the View
Provide Evidence
Explain the Reasoning
Respond with Research
Weak Approach

Some people disagree, but they are wrong.

Stronger Approach

Explain the opposing position fairly, show why a reasonable person might find it convincing, then identify its limitations and respond with evidence.

Acknowledgement and Response

Recognizing the merits of another position can strengthen your credibility because it demonstrates that you understand the broader conversation rather than ignoring inconvenient evidence.

But acknowledgement cannot be the final move. You must return to your own argument and explain how your research responds.

Do Not End on Someone Else's Argument

If you introduce an alternative viewpoint and stop there, the reader leaves the paragraph focused on the opposing idea. Respond before moving forward.

Research Presentation: Continue the Conversation

Test your understanding by explaining it to others

Research does not end when the paper is submitted. In academic, professional, and workplace settings, research often becomes a presentation, meeting, briefing, report, or conversation.

The Goal

Take what you have learned, explain it clearly to other people, and discover whether you truly understand the ideas well enough to communicate them.

Why Talking About Research Matters

Writing gives you time to revise and polish. Speaking asks you to demonstrate that the knowledge is actually yours.

  • Explaining research demonstrates understanding.
  • Questions reveal gaps you may not notice while writing.
  • Discussion exposes you to perspectives you may not have considered.
  • Presentation requires you to identify what matters most.
  • Communication turns research into something other people can use.

The Skills Do Not Change. The Format Does.

Analyze

Decide which information matters most.

Organize

Give the audience a clear path through complex ideas.

Support

Use evidence to establish credibility.

Explain

Translate scholarly ideas into understandable language.

Respond

Answer questions and address different perspectives.

Adapt

Communicate differently depending on audience and setting.

Become the Person Who Knows the Topic

By the end of the semester, you have spent weeks reading, researching, defining, and arguing about this issue. The presentation is your chance to speak from that developed knowledge rather than merely reading information off a slide.

Research Is Social

Scholarship grows because people share ideas, challenge one another, ask questions, revise conclusions, and continue the conversation. Presenting your research lets you practice that final part of the process.

The Bigger Picture

Research begins with a question, but it does not end with an answer. It continues when we share what we know and allow other people to respond.
